### 01 / About the Lab

**Homepage introduction**

At ΔMARS Lab, we investigate how robots collaborate and interact with the physical world. From autonomous cable manipulation and robotic knotting to cooperative aerial transportation and blimp systems, our research combines modeling, perception, planning, and control to develop new capabilities for autonomous robots.

**Laboratory overview**

ΔMARS Lab studies cooperative robotics, physical interaction, and autonomous manipulation. We investigate how individual robots and teams coordinate their actions, manipulate objects, and establish useful physical relationships with their environments. Our research connects dynamics, control, perception, and planning across aerial and ground platforms, including multirotor vehicles, autonomous blimps, and heterogeneous robotic teams.

Cable-based manipulation and robotic knotting form a distinctive focus within this broader agenda. Cables, ropes, and knots are not only objects to manipulate: they can become mechanisms for establishing connections, distributing forces, securing payloads, and creating mechanical constraints. We investigate autonomous cable routing, hitch and knot formation, interlacing, tension regulation, and friction-based grasping alongside broader questions of contact-rich interaction and cooperative autonomy.

Our approach combines mathematical foundations, algorithms, and experimental systems. Models guide planning and feedback, while physical experiments reveal where assumptions need refinement. This cycle connects scientific understanding with robotic behavior and supports research beyond any single mechanism or platform. Directed by Dr. Diego S. D’Antonio, the laboratory is part of Oakland University’s Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering in Rochester, Michigan.

#### Cooperative Robotics and Multi-Agent Systems

**Card title:** Cooperative Robotics

**Card description:** We study coordination, cooperative control, and collaborative manipulation using teams of autonomous robots across aerial, ground, and heterogeneous platforms.

Cooperative robotics asks how a team can achieve tasks beyond the capabilities of its individual members. We investigate coordination, cooperative control, collaborative manipulation, and the distribution of motion and physical forces. Shared objects and flexible connections can mechanically couple robots, while heterogeneous platforms introduce different capabilities and constraints. Research includes cooperative transportation, formation control, and distributed decision-making across aerial and ground systems. The challenge is to connect collective task objectives with feasible actions at each robot. This theme draws on models of interaction, perception, and planning, with cable-mediated cooperation as one distinctive area of investigation.

**Representative questions**

- How can a robot team distribute forces while maintaining a desired object motion?
- What information must robots exchange to coordinate through shared cables?
- How should cooperation adapt when a contact changes or one robot reaches an actuation limit?

**Potential applications:** Shared payload transportation, Cooperative object retrieval, Distributed handling of large or flexible structures. These are motivating use cases, not deployed capabilities.

#### Cable-Based Manipulation and Robotic Knotting

**Card title:** Cable Manipulation and Robotic Knotting

**Card description:** We investigate autonomous cable routing, hitch and knot formation, interlacing, and manipulation of flexible linear objects for grasping and securement.

Cables, ropes, and knots can act as mechanisms that enable robotic tasks. We investigate how robots route flexible linear objects, form hitches and knots, regulate tension, and exploit friction to establish attachments and secure payloads. The challenge is to connect the geometry of a cable arrangement with its mechanical behavior: a route must be achievable, and the resulting contact must support the intended task. Research includes cable interlacing, entanglement, grasp formation, and cooperative handling. This distinctive focus connects modeling, perception, planning, and control while contributing to the laboratory’s broader agenda in autonomous manipulation and physical interaction.

**Representative questions**

- How do tension, friction, and contact geometry determine whether a cable attachment supports a task?
- How can a robot team form, inspect, and release cable-based connections autonomously?

**Potential applications:** Aerial grasping, Object securement, Robotic assembly, Cooperative transportation, Flexible-object manipulation. These are motivating use cases, not deployed capabilities.

#### Physical Interaction and Contact-Rich Robotics

**Card title:** Physical Interaction

**Card description:** We study how robots model, sense, and control contact with objects and environments to perform physically constrained manipulation tasks.

Physical interaction changes how a robot can move and what it can accomplish. We investigate contact between robots, objects, and environments, including attachment, pushing, grasping, and manipulation of rigid or flexible structures. Contact may establish a useful constraint, transmit a force, or introduce uncertainty through friction and slipping. Our research connects interaction sensing with models and feedback so robots can respond to changing conditions. The scientific challenge is to plan and regulate contact as part of the task, rather than consider motion alone. This theme spans aerial and ground platforms and connects cooperative action with autonomous manipulation.

**Representative questions**

- How can robots establish and regulate useful contact while maintaining stable behavior?
- Which measurements reveal contact geometry, slipping, and interaction forces?
- How should manipulation plans adapt when contact constraints change?

#### Dynamics, Modeling, and Control

**Card title:** Dynamics and Control

**Card description:** We develop models and feedback methods for robotic systems whose behavior changes with contact, friction, cable tension, and mechanical coupling.

Robotic systems change their behavior when contacts are established, released, or rearranged. We develop models and control methods for nonlinear dynamics, hybrid systems, contact mechanics, friction, and mechanical coupling across aerial and ground platforms. Cables and suspended loads provide important examples: tension, slack, and changing geometry connect robot motion to object behavior. The challenge is to retain the physical variables that matter while keeping models useful for estimation, planning, and feedback. Stability analysis and experimental validation guide our approach. This theme supplies foundations for cooperative robotics, knotting and cable manipulation, and broader contact-rich autonomous tasks.

**Representative questions**

- Which interaction variables are necessary for a useful reduced-order model?
- How can controllers account for transitions between slack, tension, sticking, and sliding?
- What stability and feasibility conditions can guide manipulation under changing contacts?

**Potential applications:** Suspended-load control, Flexible-object handling, Interaction-aware planning and feedback. These are motivating use cases, not deployed capabilities.

#### Autonomous Perception and Planning

**Card title:** Perception and Planning

**Card description:** We connect perception, estimation, and task planning so robots can decide when to observe, move, or manipulate their surroundings.

We investigate state estimation, sensor fusion, active perception, manipulation planning, and task-level decision-making. Actions may change both an object’s position and the information available about it: moving an obstacle can reveal a target, while adjusting a cable can expose an interaction state. The challenge is to close the loop between observations, physically feasible plans, and feedback control. This theme supports aerial and cooperative tasks while leaving room for exploratory work in robotic search and object retrieval.

## Part D — Research and project storytelling

Use the sequence **question → physical mechanism → method → evidence → limitation**. Begin with what a visitor can see, introduce one technical variable, then connect it to the scientific question. Mark actual data, explanatory schematics, and proposed systems explicitly.

| Research theme | Experimental video | Scientific animation | Interactive concept | Still images |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cable manipulation and robotic knotting | Cable routing, hitch formation, attachment, and lift in one documented trial | Knot or hitch geometry and cable–object contact region | Select a stage to inspect constraints; avoid implying a validated simulator | Full-system photograph plus attachment close-up |
| Physical interaction | Actual contact establishment and regulation on aerial or ground robots | Contact regions and constraints | Inspect an illustrative contact mode with assumptions labeled | Robot–object interface and measured interaction state |
| Cooperative robotics | Shared-load motion with all robots visible | Robot trajectories and interaction-force vectors | Inspect individual roles and shared constraints | Team configuration with geometry annotations |
| Dynamics and control | A real contact or slack–tension transition synchronized to measurements | Mode changes, suspended-load response, and force directions | Inspect an idealized taut/slack diagram with assumptions shown | Interface close-up plus a readable sensor trace |
| Perception and planning | Observe–act–observe sequence, followed by retrieval when demonstrated | Visibility changes and candidate actions | Step through a labeled illustrative decision sequence | Actual camera view and estimated-state overlay |

**Project A — Cable interlacing:** Show the initial attachment problem, then the cable route and resulting contact. A continuous camera view supports understanding of the maneuver. Use a close-up to explain friction and an overhead diagram to explain routing. Preserve the original institution and authors. Do not cut together unrelated trials to imply one continuous success.

**Project B — Sliding transport:** Introduce the hook and changing cable distribution before showing robot motion. A synchronized schematic clarifies why attachment geometry matters. Pair any selected tracking trace with units, test conditions, and the authors’ explanation. Keep the preprint label visible.

**Project C — Blimp capture emulation:** Show robot roles, target motion, approach, and contact. State the terrestrial emulation scope in the caption. Add a compact assumptions panel naming buoyancy, aerodynamic drag, gravity, and actuation differences; do not label a blimp trial as orbital validation.

**Project D — Cable sensing:** Pair a physical event with its measurement. A tension trace should show actual measurements and units, with an indication of how the estimate is used. Until sensors and experiments are confirmed, use only a labeled conceptual state diagram.

**Project E — Search and manipulation:** Present an exploratory storyboard: partial observation, action choice, new observation, and retrieval attempt. Explain how an action can reveal information. Use conceptual graphics until hardware and results exist.

**Caption template:** “What is happening. Which scientific variable matters. What this experiment demonstrates under the stated conditions. Original experiment location and credited authors.” Include failure cases and limitations where they help assess the claim.

**Media formats and delivery**

- MP4 (broad support) plus WebM where useful: preferred for actual experiments and preview loops. Video gives better compression and playback control than GIF for photographic motion. Use poster images, `playsinline`, and `preload="none"` or `metadata`; mute autoplay previews and provide a pause control. Never autoplay audible content.
- GIF: reserve for very short, low-resolution explanatory snippets where video is unsuitable; avoid it for large experiment sequences.
- Animated WebP: useful for short visual snippets with a static fallback, but lacks the playback and caption controls of video.
- Lightweight SVG: preferred for technical diagrams, vector labels, and restrained schematic motion. Provide textual explanations and respect reduced-motion settings.
- JavaScript interactions: use for inspecting roles, geometry, and assumptions. Label illustrative quantities; do not invent measurements or present a toy model as experimental validation.
- YouTube: click-to-load embeds for full talks or demonstrations, with a clear iframe title and captions/transcript. Avoid loading several players at initial page load.
- Galleries: responsive image grids with meaningful captions. Bootstrap modal or the existing GLightbox can handle a lightbox after real assets arrive; keyboard access and focus restoration must be checked.

Target mobile-first assets. Give images explicit dimensions, responsive sizes, and descriptive alt text. Load below-fold media lazily. A muted loop needs an adjacent description; videos with speech need captions and a transcript. The hero must remain legible and useful before its media loads. Do not create synthetic imagery that appears to document a real experiment.
